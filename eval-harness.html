Project · AI evaluation harness

How do you know if an AI got better?

Teams change their AI models and prompts all the time. Each change might make things better or quietly make them worse. An evaluation, or "eval", is how you find out. I built a tool that runs these checks, and is careful about whether the results can be trusted.

What it is

It's an exam for an AI

The idea is the same as testing a student. Write questions with known answers, let the AI sit the exam, mark it, and look at the results.

  1. Step 1

    Write the exam

    Questions where the right answer is known in advance.

  2. Step 2

    The AI sits it three times

    An AI can answer the same question differently each time:

    escalate✓ right
    routine✗ wrong
    escalate✓ right
    model run × 3 reps
  3. Step 3

    Mark it

    Each answer is checked against the answer key, or by a second AI using a marking rubric.

  4. Step 4

    Get a report card

    A score, a range it could fall in, the cost, and the speed.

What it found

Three Claude models, 108 science questions

I gave Claude Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 the same 110 questions from seven public benchmarks, ranging from school science to research-level problems. Each model answered every question three times at the same effort setting. Two questions turned out to have faulty answer keys, so they're left out of the scores (more on that below).

Opus's safety filter flagged three PhD-level biology questions and one research-level biology question as risky, and declined them on every try. Sonnet and Haiku answered all four. Whether Opus comes first or second depends on how you count those refusals, which is why the tool reports them separately instead of hiding them inside the score.

Two "wrong" answers were the answer key's fault

excluded from every score
  • An embryology question whose answer key gives "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny", a 19th-century theory that biologists have long since abandoned. All three models chose the modern answer every time, and Opus explained why the key was wrong.
  • A school physics question about regenerative brakes that can fairly be read two ways. All three models consistently chose the reading the key doesn't accept.

Both stood out because every model "failed" them on every try, which is the pattern worth investigating. They're now listed in the suite's known-issues file: left out of the scores, but still shown in every report.

Run on 11 October 2026 at medium effort: 990 answers in total, about $7.55 including judging and a test run. Questions from GPQA and Humanity's Last Exam aren't shown, at those datasets' request.

Under the hood

Mistakes it won't count against the AI

Most misleading AI results come from the test, not the AI. The tool keeps these problems separate so they can't distort the score.

A network hiccup isn't a wrong answer

If the connection fails, the question is retried and logged separately instead of being marked wrong.

errors.jsonl · retry with backoff

A cut-off answer isn't a wrong answer

Answers that ran out of space, or that the AI declined to give, are counted and shown on their own.

status: truncated / refused

A blank answer can never pass

Saying nothing is recorded as "no answer", so it can't be mistaken for a correct "none".

answered: false

The marking is tested first

Before any AI runs, every correct answer must pass the marking and every blank must fail it.

evalh validate · oracle / null check
